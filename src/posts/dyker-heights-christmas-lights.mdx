Every December, a few square blocks of Dyker Heights in southwest Brooklyn turn into the most over-the-top Christmas display in New York. Not "tasteful lights on a nice house." A two-story Santa. A carousel in the front yard. A nativity scene that eats the whole lawn. Somewhere, a homeowner is paying an electric bill that would make you sit down.

I live in New York and I have been walking these blocks for years, but the best thing I know about Dyker Heights came from a friend who actually lives out there. I asked her which streets matter, and her answer was almost comically short.

> The most popular block is 84th St between 12th and 10th Ave. 83rd and 82nd are nice too, between those avenues. There are some more houses scattered around the neighborhood, but nearby all the homes participate from 82nd to 84th Street between 12th and 10th Avenue.

That is the entire guide. Everything below is just the logistics: how to get there, when to go, and whether you should pay someone to walk you around.

## The short version

- **The lights are free.** These are private homes. No tickets, no gate, no organizers. You walk the sidewalk like you live there.
- **The main event is 84th Street between 11th and 12th Avenue.** Lucy Spata's house is at 1152, directly across from the house the Polizzotto family used to decorate at 1145.
- **82nd and 83rd, same stretch between 12th and 10th Avenue,** are the supporting acts and they are not much less impressive.
- **Go on a weeknight if you can.** Weekend nights in mid-December, between 5 and 9 p.m., are the busiest stretch of the season.
- **Give it 90 minutes.** The loop I drew below is about 1.3 miles.

## When the lights are on

The neighborhood switches on the day after Thanksgiving, which in 2026 means Friday, November 27, and the displays run through New Year's. Going early in the season works, but the full show is usually not up until mid-December.

Lights come on around dusk, which in December means about 4:30 to 5 p.m. Most houses shut them off around 10 p.m. The sweet spot is the twenty minutes right at dusk: the sky still has color, the lights are blazing, and the sidewalks are not full yet.

If you are in town for the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade, this is an easy pairing. The parade is Thursday morning on the Upper West Side, and the Dyker Heights lights come on for the first time the next night. Thanksgiving Friday is busy but it has a holiday buzz that is hard to beat.

## Getting there

Dyker Heights has no subway station. That surprises people. You ride to the edge of the neighborhood and walk in.

**By subway (best option):** Take the R to 86th Street in Bay Ridge, then walk west on 86th Street for about 15 to 20 minutes. Alternatively, take the D to 79th Street at New Utrecht Avenue and walk north. Note that the walk in is uphill and the walk out is not.

**By bus:** The B16 and B4 run along 86th Street and the B8 cuts through the neighborhood on 18th Avenue. Useful if you are already in Brooklyn.

**By car or rideshare:** Doable, but you are driving into a residential grid that gets genuinely jammed. Street parking is competitive and traffic on the main blocks is slow. A rideshare from Manhattan runs about 40 to 60 minutes and roughly $35 to $70 depending on the night. If you take a car, get dropped at 86th Street and 12th Avenue and walk in from there.

**If you are on a bus tour:** the city runs a bus drop-off zone, a barricaded lane the police call "the chute," on 86th Street between 12th and 13th Avenue. Knowing that helps you spot where the groups unload.

## The loop

Here is the route I would walk. It hits all three core blocks, keeps you moving in one direction, and spits you back out on 86th Street near the buses and the food.

1. **86th Street and 12th Avenue.** Start here. If you booked the local walking tour, this is also their meeting point.
2. **84th Street between 12th and 11th Avenue.** The centerpiece. Lucy Spata's house at 1152 is the one that started the whole tradition: 40 light-up angels on the lawn, put up for her mother, and it has grown every year since. Across the street at 1145, the Polizzotto house has changed hands and the new owners decorate it differently, so the famous 15-foot Santa is not the sure thing it once was. The block is still the loudest thing you will see all night.
3. **82nd Street between 11th and 10th Avenue.** Quieter than 84th, which means you can actually stop and look instead of shuffling along with the crowd.
4. **83rd Street between 12th and 11th Avenue.** Look for the house at 1134, which several guides call the "Forest of Lights." One block west, between 10th and 11th, is the house locals call the "Believe House."
5. **11th Avenue south to 86th Street.** Your exit. It is a straight walk down a quiet avenue while everyone else fights for space on the main blocks.

One thing that surprises first-timers: homeowners set up tables on the sidewalk selling hot chocolate, snacks, and neighborhood T-shirts, and some houses collect for charity. Bring a few dollars and you can turn a walk into a decent snack.

## The paid tours

If you would rather not think about logistics, the tours are legitimate and some of them are good. Here is the honest comparison.

**Dyker Heights Christmas Lights (DHCL)** runs the tour on the website you have probably already found, and it is the one company that was founded by neighborhood residents. Their bus tour is $75 a seat, about four hours, with pickup and drop-off near the New York Public Library at 5th Avenue and 41st Street. It includes a 90-minute guided walk through the blocks, gifts for kids, and a drink on the way back. Their small-group walking tour is $35 and meets at 86th Street and 12th Avenue, which is step 1 of my loop. They also do private tours with a car and a guide. The 2026 season starts November 27.

**A Slice of Brooklyn** calls theirs "The Original Christmas Lights Tour of Dyker Heights" and has been running it for 17 years. Adults are $65, children under 12 are $55, it leaves from Union Square at 6 p.m. nightly through December except the 24th and 25th, and runs about three and a half hours. Their route also covers Bay Ridge and Bensonhurst, and the guides know the homeowners, which shows.

**Marketplace tours** on GetYourGuide and Viator run from about $47 to $55 for three to four hour bus tours. Read the reviews carefully and check whether they actually walk you through the blocks or just idle the bus past them.

## Tour or no tour

Do it yourself if you are comfortable on the subway, you want to go at your own pace, and you would rather spend the money on dinner. The neighborhood is safe, the streets are well lit, and the map above is all the guidance you need.

Pay for the tour if you are traveling with kids or older family, if you are visiting for a short window and do not want to burn two hours on transit, or if you want the stories behind the houses. The bus tours exist because getting there from Midtown is genuinely annoying, and that is a real problem worth paying to skip.

If you want the cheapest version of the guided experience, take the $35 walking tour. You get a local guide and the history, you skip the bus ride, and you are already standing where you need to be.

## Before you go

- **Use a bathroom before you arrive.** There are essentially none in the residential blocks.
- **Stay on the sidewalk and stay off the lawns.** People live here all year, and the driveways stay in use.
- **Dress one layer warmer than you think.** You will be standing still outdoors for over an hour.
- **Bring a battery pack.** The photos eat your phone alive.
- **Do not block the crosswalks.** This is the number one complaint from residents.
- **Eat before or after, not during.** The blocks are residential. Head to 13th Avenue, the neighborhood's main street, for old-school bakeries like Grandma's Bakery between 72nd and 73rd Streets, or Krispy Pizza at 7112 13th Avenue. Bay Ridge's 3rd and 5th Avenues have the better full dinner options if you are willing to walk another 15 minutes.

## If you have one more stop in you

Locals will tell you about Sam the Greek's house, which is a few blocks southwest of the loop around 71st Street and 14th Avenue. It is the one with roughly 295,000 lights. It is not part of the main display area, so treat it as a bonus stop on the way back to the train.
