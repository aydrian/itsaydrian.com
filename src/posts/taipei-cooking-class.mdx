I spent three hours in a Taipei kitchen learning to make Taiwan's three most famous foods: beef noodle soup, bubble tea and xiao long bao. The class was at CookInn Taiwan, there were about ten of us and one instructor, and we had three dishes to get through before lunch. My brother's family takes a cooking class on every trip, so I booked this one for my week in Taipei.

Here is what the class is like, the tips the instructor gave us for each dish, and how to book it. The full recipes are in the cookbook they hand you at the end, so I'll leave those to CookInn.

## Beef noodle soup

First up was beef noodle soup, and it starts with the cut. The instructor uses beef shank, for two reasons: it's lean and it's cheap.

The tips that stuck with me:

- **The spice bag does the heavy lifting.** Sichuan peppercorn, dried mandarin peel and a few more spices get tied up in a little pouch called a lubao. You can buy one ready-made at a Chinese medicine store. They're cheap and make a nice souvenir.
- **Tie the scallions together.** You only want their flavor, not the scallions themselves, and a tied bundle is easy to pick out at the end. The white part of the scallion gets stir-fried first and works like another spice.
- **Use a cheap rice wine.** She was very clear about this one. No need for an expensive bottle.
- **There are two bean pastes.** Chili bean paste is the spicy one, and a milder soybean paste is there for anyone who doesn't like heat. She offered us a taste of the paste on its own. I tried it. My face in the video says enough.
- **Soy sauce goes in last.** Never cook your soy sauce too long. This was the tip I hadn't heard before.
- **Pickled mustard greens go on top.** They make the soup less greasy, and she says they help with digestion too.

## Bubble tea

Next was bubble tea, with a fun fact I didn't see coming. In Chinese, the word for bubble sounds a lot like the word for foam. So "bubble tea" was first named for the foam on top of shaken tea, not for the pearls. The pearls came later.

She told us about the tea houses of the '70s and '80s, where people would sit for hours with friends over glasses of tea the size of a fish tank, instead of typing on their phones.

Then came the pearls. Tapioca won out over every other topping, she said, because of its "QQness." QQ means chewy and bouncy, and it was my first Taiwanese word of the day. Raw pearls are just starch, and they're so fragile that she crushed one between two fingers to show us. Someone joked that she'd ruined it, but she did it on purpose.

The shaking is the fun part. You shake the tea hard with lots of ice, and that is what makes the foam. She says it also brings out the tea's own sweetness, even without syrup.

## Xiao long bao

And then the main event: xiao long bao, Taiwan's famous soup dumplings.

The filling is very mild when you taste it. It smelled so good that I wanted to add an extra spoon of seasoning. The answer was no.

Then came the wrappers, which is where it gets technical. The instructor only lets you roll the pin forward, never back and forth, and you'll soon see why. Here's the method:

1. Roll the pin forward and stop two-thirds of the way across the dough.
2. Lift the pin, turn the dough, and roll two-thirds again. Repeat all the way around.
3. Thin out the edges like a sunny-side-up egg, and leave the center thicker. The center becomes the bottom of the dumpling, so it has to be thick enough to hold the soup.
4. Keep going until the wrapper is about 9 centimeters (3.5 inches) across.
